Recommended stack

Keep the technology familiar; add structure.

Use one opt-in, searchable directory; turn the existing Slack into a deliberately hosted community; and run a 60-minute sequence of small-group conversations in Google Meet. This solves the three distinct jobs without asking every attendee to learn a new venue.

Directory: Google Form → attendee-only Google Sheet, or Airtable if a card-style view matters.
2
Asynchronous: existing Slack with five purposeful channels, named discussion hosts, and a short prompt calendar.
3
Live: Google Meet breakout rooms—three 12-minute rounds in groups of four, using directory interests for one round.

01

What the evidence says

Design the social system, not just the room

Research and experience reports converge on a few durable lessons. ACM’s guide explicitly treats fewer than 200 people as a small conference / large workshop and proposes a video meeting plus Slack, with small random groups during unstructured sessions.1

01

Structure beats hope

Virtual “coffee breaks” rarely self-start. Put social events in the official programme, assign a prompt and a host, and make the first interaction easy.

02

Small groups create airtime

Groups of 3–5 let everyone speak. Combine purposeful matching with one random or cross-topic round to avoid reinforcing existing clusters.

03

Choice matters

Offer speaking, text, camera-off and asynchronous routes. Keep networking opt-in and provide a genuine break for people who need one.

04

Connections need a bridge

The directory makes people discoverable; the live session lowers the cost of first contact; Slack carries the conversation beyond the hour.

Useful empirical anchor: MIT’s Minglr deployments produced 80 ad-hoc conversations at a 275-person conference; 76% of registered users were successfully matched, but the most common reason for non-use was simply wanting a break.3 Treat networking as attractive and scheduled—not compulsory.
02

Layer one

A participant directory people can actually use

The directory should answer three questions quickly: “Who is here?”, “Who shares my interests?”, and “How may I contact them?” Collect only what will be shown, require explicit opt-in, and remove the directory after a stated period.

Recommended

Google Form → restricted Sheet

Cost: no likely incremental cost.

  • Lowest setup burden; works with the conference’s Google stack.
  • Filter by topic, career stage, timezone and “asks/offers.”
  • Share the response view only with registered attendee accounts or a Google Group.

Trade-off: utilitarian rather than beautiful; access control can create friction for non-Google identities.

Best presentation

Airtable form + shared view

Cost: free for a link-only view; $24 for one month of Team if password protection is needed.

  • Card/gallery views and strong filters make browsing pleasant.
  • Free plan permits 1,000 records per base and unlimited read-only collaborators.8
  • Shared views are excluded from search engines, but anyone with the link can open them; password protection is paid-only.

Trade-off: one more service and privacy agreement; a random link is not strong access control.

Minimal

Slack introductions only

Cost: already available.

  • Ask every attendee to post from a fixed template in #introductions.
  • Replies and DMs start naturally.
  • No roster synchronization.

Trade-off: difficult to scan and systematically filter; on Slack Free, only 90 days are searchable and data older than a year is deleted.9

Copy-ready profile fields

Name · displayed name
Affiliation & role · one line
Timezone · UTC offset + region
Career stage · optional, broad categories
Three topic tags · controlled list plus “other”
What I can offer · 140 characters
What I’m looking for · 140 characters
Current question/project · 240 characters
Contact route · Slack / email / website, participant’s choice
Opt-ins · directory, matching, mentoring—separate checkboxes

Do not collect personal email for display by default. Keep the raw form response file organizer-only; publish a curated sheet/view containing only fields participants agreed to share. Google Forms now supports responder access for individual accounts or Google Groups.7

03

Layer two

Turn Slack into an event, not a filing cabinet

A new forum will split attention. The better move is to use the existing Slack, reduce channel sprawl, and schedule a handful of visible rituals. Tools do not create discussion; prompts, hosts, and response norms do.

Five channels are enough

#start-here
Schedule, norms, help, directory link.

#introductions
Same short profile template for everyone.

#asks-and-offers
Specific requests, skills, datasets, collaborations.

#ai-metascience-ideas
One idea per top-level post; discussion in threads.

#hallway
Informal links, reactions, photos, lightweight chat.

Add topic channels only after demand appears. Empty channels make the community feel empty.

7 days before

Open with visible hosts

Organizers and speakers seed introductions first. Ask each person to reply to two others: one shared interest, one unexpected connection.

5 days before

Post the “unsolved problem” prompt

“What bottleneck in AI-enabled meta-science would you most like help with?” One idea per thread; add reactions to signal interest.

2 days before

Publish an asks/offers digest

A human curator highlights 8–12 complementary asks and offers and tags people—with consent, never as an automated surprise.

Conference day

Route live conversations back to Slack

Every breakout receives one thread for links and next steps. The facilitator reminds participants to DM or reply before leaving.

1–2 days after

Close with commitments

Prompt: “Who will you follow up with, and what is the smallest next action?” Post a short public-to-attendees summary.

Optional “idea market”: use one Miro board only if spatial clustering and voting add real value. Miro Starter is $8/month per organizer billed yearly and allows free no-account visitor editing; the free tier does not allow visitor editing.10 A simple Google Sheet with columns “idea / evidence / help wanted / owner” is usually enough.
04

Layer three

A live session engineered for useful first contact

Recommended default: one focused 60-minute Google Meet, repeated in a second timezone if the audience is global. Use groups of four—not pairs for the whole hour and not rooms of ten—and change the organizing principle each round.

00–06
Welcome + norms
Explain the three rounds, camera-optional participation, confidentiality, and the one concrete output: one follow-up contact.
06–18
Round 1 · shared interests
Pre-matched groups of four. Prompt: “What are you working on, and what question is alive for you?”
18–30
Round 2 · cross-pollination
Shuffle across topic or seniority. Prompt: “What assumption in your area would benefit from an outsider’s view?”
30–42
Round 3 · asks & offers
Prompt: “Name one thing you can help with and one thing you need.” A volunteer records only opt-in connections.
42–53
Self-selected topic rooms
Offer 5–7 named rooms, including “quiet follow-up / exchange contacts” and “take a real break.”
53–60
Commit + close
Participants post one next step in Slack; organizers share follow-up link and 2-question pulse survey.
Facilitation

Staff it visibly

One lead host, one breakout operator, one help/moderation host, plus 6–8 roaming facilitators if possible. Rooms receive the prompt in chat and a 2-minute warning.

Inclusion

Make the rules explicit

Use “step up / step back”; invite concise turns; allow camera-off and chat; do not record breakout rooms; include a leave/rejoin route and an actual break option.

Meet caveat

Check the edition

Meet can create up to 100 breakout rooms, but creation is edition-dependent. Participant caps are 100, 150 or 500 by Workspace edition; hosts count as connections.56

06

Trust, access and failure modes

Guardrails are part of the experience

Networking asks participants to make themselves visible. Protect that choice, minimize friction, and maintain a low-tech path through every activity.

Privacy and consent

Separate consent to appear in the directory from consent to be algorithmically or manually matched. State who can see the data, why it is collected, when it is deleted, and how to withdraw. Never publish participant contact details on an open web page.

Accessibility

Send prompts in advance; use captions; allow camera-off/chat responses; avoid mandatory drag-and-drop or avatar navigation; provide a plain-link alternative. Automated captions can struggle with specialist vocabulary, so share key terms and correct important records.1

Safety and moderation

Publish conduct norms and a private help route. Assign a named responder. Give hosts clear removal/escalation authority. Do not record small-group networking; tell participants before rooms open.

Capacity and technology

A meeting cap of exactly 100 leaves no room for hosts if all participants attend. Confirm the Meet edition, run a 20-person pilot, prepare static room assignments, and keep a fallback main-room activity.

Status and hierarchy

Do not let seniority determine airtime. Cross seniority deliberately, but also offer an early-career-only room. Use equal timed introductions and “step up / step back” guidance.

Timezone and fatigue

Repeat the live session at two times or record only the plenary instructions—not breakouts. Schedule networking as programme time, not as a sacrifice of the only real break.

07

Two-week implementation

A small operational plan

This can be launched without procurement. The critical work is assigning owners, getting early participation from visible people, and rehearsing the breakout transitions.

  1. T−14 days · decide and assign.
    Confirm Meet edition/capacity, choose Google Sheet or Airtable, appoint directory, Slack and live-session owners.
  2. T−12 · build and privacy-test.
    Create the opt-in form, curated directory view, deletion date and withdrawal route. Test with an external account.
  3. T−10 · simplify Slack.
    Create/rename the five channels, pin one start-here post, publish conduct norms and seed organizer introductions.
  4. T−7 · open the directory.
    Invite profiles, showing a good example. Ask speakers and organizers to participate early to create social proof.
  5. T−5 · build assignments.
    Create round 1 from shared topics, round 2 for cross-pollination, and randomize round 3 while avoiding repeated pairs.
  6. T−3 · rehearse.
    Run a full transition test with 15–20 volunteers; verify captions, timing, help requests, mobile access and the fallback.
  7. T−1 · send one clear brief.
    Share the single Meet link, exact purpose, accessibility options, prompts, directory link and Slack follow-up location.
  8. T+1 day · close the loop.
    Send connections/digest, run a 2-question survey, publish next steps, and schedule data deletion.
Minimum evaluation: ask (1) “Did you make at least one connection you intend to follow up?” and (2) “Which format should we keep/change?” Track directory opt-in, introduction posts, live attendance, completed rounds and follow-up intent—avoid vanity metrics such as raw message count.
