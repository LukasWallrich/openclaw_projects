Research design, sampling methods and implications for inferences

Correct statistical teaching errors before classroom use

The package is well organised but its sample-size answer key approximately halves the required recruitment. It also teaches observed post-hoc power and conflates low power with an inflated Type I error rate.

What works

Issues and actions

CriticalVerified calculation and answer-key error

Correct recruitment advice: approximately 70 participants per group

L1 PDF/DOCX pp.2–3, effect-size exercise

Using the stated n1=25, n2=30, SDs 3.5 and 2.8, pooled SD=3.1364 and Cohen’s d=0.47826. A two-sided independent-groups t test at alpha=.05 and power=.80 needs n=69.604 per group, rounded to 70 each (140 total). The supplied 30+35 allocation has only about 47.3% power, verified with pwr::pwr.t2n.test. Even accepting d=.495, approximately 65 is a per-group figure, not a total.

“effect size ... 0.495”; n=65 “total number in both groups ... 30 ... and 35”.

Action: Replace the full answer key with a worked pooled-SD calculation, specify test/tails/allocation, distinguish per-group/total N, and update all related slide notes. Treat recruitment advice as a release blocker.

Official pwr documentation: n is observations per sample

Replace observed post-hoc power with informative appraisal

Slides 62 and 84; slide 62 notes; L3 p.2 published-study critique

“Calculate Post-Hoc power”.

Lakens (2022), Sample Size Justification

Evidence confidence: high

MajorStatistical error

Separate low power from Type I error inflation

Slides 66–67 and corresponding notes; slide 56

For a valid prespecified test, alpha controls the false-positive probability under the null. Low power can increase the share of significant claims that are false under assumptions about base rates, and selected estimates can be exaggerated; it does not itself inflate alpha.

“Inflated Type I error rates” listed as a consequence of low power.

Action: Separate Type I error, false discovery proportion, selection/publication bias and effect-size exaggeration. Show how p-hacking or violated assumptions, rather than low power alone, inflate the Type I error rate.

Lakens (2022), Sample Size Justification

Evidence confidence: high
